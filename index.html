    Original Article

    Colonoscopy Intervals and Colorectal Cancer Incidence After Adenoma Removal

    Rodrigo Jover, Hospital General Universitario Dr. Balmis, Alicante, Spain, and co-authors

    01:17
    Speaker 1

    After removal of polyps at colonoscopy, some patients are considered to be at high risk for new polyps or cancer. For such patients, current guidelines in the United States, as well as international guidelines, recommend frequent colonoscopies for surveillance, with the first surveillance colonoscopy at three years after polyp removal. However, the recommended intervals for surveillance are based on data from observational or small randomized studies, and the guidelines emphasize the need for long-term, high-quality randomized trials.

    In this ongoing non-inferiority trial conducted in eight European countries, more than 10,000 patients with high-risk adenomas — defined as one or more adenoma with a diameter of 10 mm or greater,

    02:32

    high-grade dysplasia, or villous growth, or 3 to 10 adenomas of any kind — were randomly assigned to undergo a first colonoscopy at 5 years after polyp removal, or at the current guideline-recommended 3 years.

    At this interim analysis of a 10-year trial, the incidence of colorectal cancer at 5 years was low and did not appear to be higher in the 5-year group than in the 3-year group. The interim analysis showed non-inferiority of 5-year surveillance to 3-year surveillance among patients with high-risk adenomas.

    Design
    Non-inferiority RCT, 8 European countries
    Population
    >10,000 patients with high-risk adenomas
    Comparison
    5-year vs. 3-year first surveillance
    Interim result
    5-year interval non-inferior at 5 years
    02:32

    Aasma Shaukat from the NYU Grossman School of Medicine, New York, and Theodore Levin from the Kaiser Permanente Division of Research, Pleasanton, California, write in an editorial that the results by Jover and co-authors should prompt reconsideration of the premise in post-polypectomy care that patients who are designated as high-risk uniformly benefit from surveillance at three years. The designation itself is broad.

    03:38

    It groups together patients with one large adenoma, villous histologic features, high-grade dysplasia, or 3 to 10 adenomas. It has implied a level of future cancer risk that is sufficient to justify intensive follow-up. But the findings from the trial by Jover and co-authors suggest that the absolute risk of colorectal cancer after a high-quality baseline colonoscopy with complete excision of polyps is lower than the term "high risk" conveys. That conclusion is consistent with data from evolving literature.

    For decades, surveillance after adenoma removal has been guided by a seemingly reasonable instinct: more vigilance must be better. This trial offers an important corrective. In contemporary practice, more frequent colonoscopy may detect more lesions without meaningfully reducing the incidence of colorectal cancer over five years.

    — Editorial, Shaukat & Levin
    04:39

    Whether that conclusion will persist through the full 10 years of this study is uncertain, but these interim findings make one point clear already: surveillance policy must be determined not by habit or by fear of doing too little, but by evidence of what benefits patients.

    Original Article

    Sodium Bicarbonate for Critically Ill Adults with Metabolic Acidosis and Shock

    The SODa-BIC investigators and the ANZICS Clinical Trials Group

    04:39
    Speaker 1

    Metabolic acidosis is common in critically ill patients and is associated with organ dysfunction and death. Sodium bicarbonate is used to correct acidemia, but its benefit in patients with metabolic acidosis who are receiving vasopressors has remained uncertain.

    In this trial conducted in 55 ICUs across seven countries, 500 adult patients with metabolic acidosis who were receiving vasopressors were randomly assigned to receive either sodium bicarbonate or placebo, 5% dextrose.

    05:48

    Sodium bicarbonate or placebo was infused for up to 5 hours, with the infusion rate adjusted for a target pH of at least 7.3 and base excess of at least 0 mmol per liter. The primary outcome was a major adverse kidney event, defined as death, use of renal replacement therapy, or persistent renal dysfunction within 30 days.

    The use of sodium bicarbonate in these critically ill patients with metabolic acidosis receiving vasopressors did not lead to a lower risk of major adverse kidney events within 30 days than placebo.

    In an editorial, Marlies Ostermann from Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust, London, and Deborah Cook from McMaster University, Hamilton, Ontario, Canada, write that the rigorous SODa-BIC trial adds to the growing evidence about sodium bicarbonate treatment. Whether, when, and in whom sodium bicarbonate therapy confers benefit for patients with metabolic acidosis remains under active investigation.

    06:55

    As clinical evidence evolves, the SODa-BIC trial adds a compelling new chapter to the latest book. But the full story is not yet written.

    Original Article

    Blinatumomab for Replacing Chemotherapy in Pediatric Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia

    Martin Schrappe, Christian-Albrechts University, Kiel, Germany, and co-authors

    06:55
    Speaker 1

    Blinatumomab, a bispecific T-cell engager targeting the CD19 antigen on B cells, may offer an option to safely replace cycles of traditional chemotherapy in pediatric patients with newly diagnosed high-risk B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia.

    In this trial, 709 children with high-risk B-cell ALL were randomly assigned to receive two cycles of blinatumomab or two cycles of chemotherapy after consolidation. Among these children, replacement of two cycles of highly toxic conventional chemotherapy with blinatumomab resulted in a significantly greater percentage of patients with event-free survival at four years.

    Enrollment
    709 children, high-risk B-cell ALL
    Intervention
    2 cycles blinatumomab vs. 2 cycles chemo
    Primary gain
    Higher 4-year event-free survival
    Neurotoxicity
    12% vs. 3.2%
    08:07

    The side-effect profile was more favorable with blinatumomab than with intensive chemotherapy. Neurotoxic effects related to the trial treatment occurred in a significantly higher percentage of patients in the blinatumomab group than in the control group, 12% versus 3.2%.

    In an editorial, Regina Myers and Stephen Hunger from the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia write that this large trial enrolled children with high-risk ALL, an optimal population for evaluating blinatumomab substitution, given their historically poor outcomes and pronounced burden of treatment-related toxic effects. In the current trial, the size of the effect of blinatumomab on event-free survival, hazard ratio 0.51, mirrored the results of trials in other populations, which further establishes blinatumomab as a new standard of care in B-cell ALL. The results pave the way for future efforts to replace toxic chemotherapy components with blinatumomab as first-line therapy in B-cell ALL.

    Original Article

    Trial of a Maternal Diet Rich in Eggs and Peanuts to Reduce Infant Allergy

    Debra Palmer, The Kids Research Institute Australia, Nedlands, and co-authors

    09:21
    Speaker 1

    Immune responses leading to food allergy may develop early in life, potentially before the introduction of solid foods. Exposures to food allergens in utero and through breast milk have been proposed as a strategy to prevent food allergies in infants, but evidence from randomized trials is lacking.

    In this trial, more than 2,100 pregnant women whose unborn child had at least two biologic family members with medically diagnosed allergic disease were randomly assigned to follow either a diet high in eggs and peanuts that included at least 6 eggs and 60 peanuts per week, or a standard egg and peanut diet that included no more than 3 eggs and 30 peanuts per week, from before 23 weeks' gestation until four months postnatally during lactation.

    10:29

    The ingestion of high amounts of egg and peanut during pregnancy and lactation did not result in a significantly lower risk of the development of egg or peanut allergy in infants by one year of age than ingestion of low amounts of these foods.

    Maria Jenmalm from Linköping University, Sweden, writes in an editorial that for clinicians the message is practical. Pregnant and lactating women who tolerate egg and peanut need not avoid these foods for allergy prevention. Equally, they should not feel obliged to consume high quantities of egg or peanut to protect their child. This message is especially important for families with strong allergic histories who may already have anxiety about infant allergy risk.

    The trial supports counseling that is permissive rather than prescriptive.

    — Editorial, Maria Jenmalm
    11:22

    Maintain a nutritionally adequate, varied diet according to preference and tolerance, and focus prevention advice on timely introduction and ongoing inclusion of allergenic foods in the infant diet when developmentally appropriate. Prevention of food allergy remains an early-life priority, but this trial suggests that the actionable window is not enlarged simply by increasing maternal intake of eggs and peanuts.

    Review Article

    Esophageal Cancer

    Shao-Hua Xie, Karolinska Institutet, Stockholm, and co-authors

    11:22
    Speaker 1

    Cancer of the esophagus is the 13th most common cancer and the 7th leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, resulting in approximately 500,000 deaths annually. Population-based 5-year survival has improved in recent decades, but rarely exceeds 20%. The main histologic types of esophageal cancer, adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma, have different incidence patterns.

    12:29

    The past four decades have witnessed a rapidly increasing incidence of esophageal adenocarcinoma in many Western countries, especially among white men, and a decreasing incidence of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. In contrast, the opposite is true for most other populations, in which esophageal squamous cell carcinoma remains the most frequent form. This review focuses on recent developments in the epidemiologic characteristics, risk factors, prevention, and clinical management of esophageal cancer.

    Case Record of the MGH

    A 62-Year-Old Woman with Memory Loss, Anhedonia, and Temporal Wasting

    Sejal Shah and colleagues, Massachusetts General Hospital

    12:29
    Speaker 1

    A 62-year-old woman receiving treatment for metastatic breast cancer presented with a month of profound loss of appetite, altered taste, fatigue, and weight loss, followed by worsening memory and difficulty walking.

    13:31

    Previously able to walk two to three miles, she became too tired and unsteady to walk more than a block and eventually needed walls and furniture for support. Her son noticed increasing confusion and short-term memory loss, and she became disengaged from activities she had enjoyed. Examination revealed temporal wasting, severe memory impairment, and confabulation.

    Her rapid weight loss and poor oral intake raised concerns for thiamine deficiency, and repeat brain MRI showed abnormalities of the mammillary bodies consistent with Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. She received high-dose intravenous thiamine with some improvement in appetite and cognition, although memory impairment persisted after discharge.

    Perspective

    Artificial Intelligence and the Future of the Clinical Workforce

    Dhruv Khullar, Weill Cornell Medical College, New York

    13:31
    Speaker 1

    Concerns about technology disrupting the work of clinicians have existed for decades, but they have intensified in recent years amid the growing sophistication of AI tools and their rapid adoption in health care.

    14:30

    AI has increasingly outperformed clinicians on complex clinical tasks, and the federal government recently announced an initiative to develop agents that can autonomously provide care to patients. Some experts have predicted that AI will take over clinical tasks to such an extent that there will be fewer employment opportunities for clinicians, especially in specialties such as radiology, pathology, psychiatry, and primary care. Counter-arguments often emphasize technological, regulatory, and humanistic factors.

    15:20

    It is possible, for example, that AI agents won't be as effective in the real world as they have been in benchmarking studies, that regulatory requirements will slow the implementation of AI tools or limit their applications, and that patients will continue to highly value empathic, relational care.

    Although each of these considerations has merit, economic theory and history offer distinct and possibly more structural reasons to believe that in the long run, adoption of AI agents — even models capable of performing some forms of cognitive work — could lead to an expansion rather than a contraction of the clinical workforce. Developing a clearer sense of the range of possible futures for AI and the clinical workforce is an essential step in bringing about the future we want.

    A New Approach for the Indian Health Service: Academic Physician Staffing

    Matthew Tobey, Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston; Allison Kelliher, Association of American Indian Physicians, Oklahoma City

    16:15
    Speaker 1

    The Indian Health Service (IHS) provides health care to citizens of federally recognized American Indian and Alaska Native tribes, fulfilling a government responsibility established by means of treaties, laws, U.S. Supreme Court decisions, and other precedents. Nearly 2 million people, predominantly people living in rural, remote, and low-income areas, receive care at IHS hospitals and health centers.

    In 2018, the Government Accountability Office reported regional IHS physician vacancy rates of 20 to 46 percent. One potentially scalable approach to addressing long-standing IHS physician shortages has received relatively little attention: staffing partnerships with academic medical centers (AMCs).

    17:21

    Pilot staffing partnerships between the IHS and AMCs were first launched in the mid-2010s, and since then additional IHS–AMC programs have been established. If today's IHS–AMC partnerships represent the first phase of a new approach to addressing IHS staffing shortages, these authors believe the time has come for a second phase focused on sustainability and scale.

    Perspective

    The Epstein Files Transparency Act and the Health of Trafficked Children

    Nicholas Peoples, Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston

    17:21
    Speaker 1

    Human trafficking is a complex power system that functions as both a proximate and distal cause of poor child health. Yet health systems' responses remain focused on identifying and treating individual victims after the greatest harms have already occurred, rather than partnering with communities and policymakers to address the structural drivers of exploitation. The Epstein Files Transparency Act, signed into U.S. law in November 2025, brought these tensions to the fore.

    18:38

    Among the records that have been released are accounts of adolescent girls transported across jurisdictions and international borders, controlled by adults with financial and social power, and subjected to repeated sexual exploitation. In some cases, these minors interacted with health care systems where no one recognized their circumstances.

    The Act offers a rare opportunity to identify perpetrators, pursue accountability and punishment, and thereby prevent further emboldening of child trafficking. However, given that policymakers vary in their willingness to investigate this global trafficking network and hold suspected perpetrators accountable, the medical and public health communities face a choice.

    19:41

    Treat human trafficking as an unfortunate issue that lies outside its domain, or confront it as a health crisis that demands public health intervention.

    Perspective

    Honoring the Legacy of the HIV Epidemic

    Ofole Mgbako, NYC Health + Hospitals / Bellevue, New York

    19:41
    Speaker 1

    After decades of scientific progress, HIV is now a treatable illness, and many HIV prevention tools are available. Yet progress is stalling. In New York City, the incidence of new HIV infections has increased over the past two years, and more than 1,500 people with HIV die each year, with a third of those deaths being AIDS-related.

    Our treatments are so effective today that these deaths are almost universally deaths of despair related to homelessness, or drug use, or mental illness, or to drowning under the weight of persistent stigma that keeps some patients from obtaining treatment. HIV has essentially been, and continues to be, an epidemic of inequity.

    20:37

    To make matters worse, HIV prevention and treatment programs have been subject to sustained attacks by the U.S. government for more than a year, including threats to Medicaid access and termination of more than $200 million in HIV-related research funding. And the author also sees an even greater threat to progress against the epidemic: fear. The fear of immigrant patients with HIV that coming to clinic for care may mean being arrested by ICE. The effects of fear on the rates of new HIV infections and related deaths are impossible to fully measure epidemiologically.

    Clinicians and patients continue to grapple with the legacy of the HIV epidemic: the heavy losses, the creative activism that grew out of it, the lessons about public health, and the kind of dignified care patients deserve even in the absence of a cure. Yet our models of care have never revolved only around numbers.

    They've been grounded in quiet, persistent courage — the capacity to see someone suffering and lean in rather than turn away. Fearlessness, and innovation, may be our greatest inheritance.
